H.R. 4400
Representative Nikhil Kyadiggeri introduced the following bill, which was referred to the Committee on Energy and Environment.

A BILL

To establish the Water, Accountability, Transparency, and Tracking Standards for Utility Protection Act.

Be it enacted by the House of Representatives and the United States Senate in Congress assembled, that:

Purpose & Goals ▾

Whereas data centers consumed about 4.4 percent of all electricity in the United States in 2023, and the Department of Energy's Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory projects that they could consume between 6.7 and 12 percent by 2028 as artificial intelligence grows; whereas the cost of building the new power plants, substations, and transmission lines needed to serve these facilities is often spread across every utility customer, raising electric bills for families and small businesses that never use a data center; whereas large data centers also consumed roughly 66 billion liters of water for cooling in 2023; and whereas the United States must continue to lead the world in artificial intelligence rather than push this industry overseas, be it enacted that the growth of data centers shall be transparent, shall pay its own way, and shall become cleaner and more efficient over time without slowing American innovation.

Plain English: Data centers are using a fast-growing slice of the country's electricity and water, and the cost of hooking them up usually lands on everyone's bill. This bill makes the industry transparent, self-funding, and cleaner — without chasing it out of the country.

Section 1: Short Title and Definitions ▾

This Act may be cited as the "Water, Accountability, Transparency, and Tracking Standards for Utility Protection Act," or the "WATTS UP Act." For the purposes of this Act:

  1. "Large data center" means any facility, or group of facilities on the same site, that stores or processes digital data and has a peak electricity demand of 50 megawatts (MW) or more, which is roughly enough power for 40,000 homes;
  2. "Clean energy" means electricity generated from solar, wind, hydroelectric, geothermal, or nuclear sources, or from any other source that produces no carbon dioxide emissions; and
  3. "Power usage effectiveness" (PUE) means the total energy used by a data center divided by the energy used only by its computing equipment, so that a PUE of 1.0 would be perfectly efficient.
Plain English: The bill only applies to the giants (50 MW and up). PUE is the industry's standard efficiency score: the closer to 1.0, the less energy is wasted on cooling and overhead.

Section 2: Public Reporting of Energy and Water Use ▾

Beginning one year after this Act takes effect, the owner or operator of every large data center shall report to the Department of Energy once each year:

  1. the total electricity it used, measured in megawatt-hours, and its highest peak demand;
  2. the total water it consumed for cooling, measured in gallons;
  3. its average PUE for the year; and
  4. the share of its electricity that came from clean energy.

The Department of Energy shall publish these reports in a free, searchable public database within 90 days of receiving them, so that the public, state regulators, and Congress know exactly how much energy and water this industry is using. The Department may protect information that would reveal a company's trade secrets, but it may not hide the totals. Any owner or operator that fails to report, or that knowingly reports false information, shall pay a civil penalty of up to $10,000 for each day the violation continues.

Plain English: Right now nobody outside the companies really knows these numbers. This section creates a public scoreboard, protects legitimate trade secrets, and fines anyone who hides or fakes their data.

Section 3: Ratepayer Protection — Data Centers Pay Their Own Way ▾

Within one year of this Act taking effect, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) shall establish a national large-load standard, and every state public utility commission shall apply it to the utilities it regulates. Under this standard:

  1. a large data center shall pay the full cost of any new power plants, substations, and transmission lines that a utility must build in order to serve it, instead of having those costs added to the bills of other customers;
  2. before a utility connects a large data center, the data center shall sign a contract of at least 12 years and shall pay an exit fee if it closes early, so that households are never left paying for equipment that is no longer needed; and
  3. no utility may raise the rates of residential or small business customers in order to recover the cost of serving a large data center.

This section builds on the Ratepayer Protection Act (H.R. 9340), which passed the House of Representatives 417 to 3 on September 16, 2026, but only asked states to consider such a standard. This Act makes the standard a requirement.

Plain English: If a utility has to build new infrastructure to serve a data center, the data center pays for it — not you. The 12-year contract and exit fee stop a company from leaving town and sticking residents with the bill for a half-used substation.

Section 4: Efficiency and Clean Energy Standards ▾

The Department of Energy shall set and enforce the following standards:

  1. every large data center that begins construction after January 1, 2028 shall be designed to operate at a PUE of 1.3 or better, and existing large data centers shall reach a PUE of 1.5 or better by 2032;
  2. every large data center shall obtain at least 50 percent of its annual electricity from clean energy by 2030 and 100 percent by 2035, and at least half of that clean energy must come from new power sources built after this Act is passed, so that data centers add clean power to the grid instead of taking it away from existing customers; and
  3. every large data center shall sign a demand-response agreement with its utility under which it agrees to reduce its electricity use by at least 10 percent during a grid emergency, such as a heat wave, in exchange for a credit on its electric bill.

A large data center that cannot meet a deadline in this section may apply to the Department of Energy for a one-time extension of up to two years if it shows a detailed plan for compliance.

Plain English: Efficiency targets the industry's best facilities already meet, a clean-energy timeline with nine years of runway, and a deal where data centers dial down during heat waves so the grid doesn't fail. If a company is honestly trying and falls behind, it can get two more years.

Section 5: Funding, Enforcement, and Effective Date ▾

Funds for this Act shall be provided by a Grid Accountability Fee of $1.00 per megawatt-hour of electricity used by large data centers, which equals about one-tenth of one cent per kilowatt-hour. Based on the roughly 176 million megawatt-hours that data centers used in 2023, this fee would raise approximately $176 million per year and would rise as the industry grows, without costing ordinary households anything. The money shall be deposited in a Data Center Energy Accountability Fund at the Department of the Treasury and spent as follows:

  1. $25 million per year to the Department of Energy to run the reporting database and enforce this Act;
  2. $100 million per year in grants to utilities and local governments in communities that host large data centers, to strengthen their power grids and lower bills for residents; and
  3. the remainder in rebates to large data centers that beat the efficiency and clean energy standards in Section 4 ahead of schedule, so that companies are rewarded for doing the right thing early.

The Department of Energy may fine any large data center that violates Section 3 or Section 4 up to $50,000 per day, and the Government Accountability Office shall report to Congress every two years on whether this Act is working. This Act shall take effect 180 days after it is signed into law.

Plain English: The industry funds its own oversight with a tiny fee — a tenth of a penny per kilowatt-hour. Most of the money flows back to the towns that host data centers, and companies that move fast get some of it back as rebates. Taxpayers pay nothing.

Conclusion ▾

Artificial intelligence is going to be a huge part of our future, and the data centers that power it are going to be built somewhere. The question this bill answers is who pays for them and how responsibly they are run. Through the passage of the WATTS UP Act, the public will finally be able to see how much energy and water this industry uses, families and small businesses will be protected from paying for power lines and plants they never asked for, and the companies building these facilities will be pushed to make them as efficient and as clean as possible. This bill does not slow down American innovation; it makes sure that innovation does not come at the expense of the people who live next door to it.
