The problem

The electricity bill for the AI boom is being split with people who never asked for it.

Data centers — the warehouses full of computers that run AI, cloud storage, and streaming — more than doubled their power use between 2017 and 2023, and the Department of Energy expects them to double or triple again by 2028.

To serve a single large facility, a utility often has to build new power plants, substations, and transmission lines. Under most current rules, that cost gets folded into everyone's rates. Families and small businesses in San Diego end up subsidizing the power supply of some of the richest companies on Earth.

In September 2026 the House passed the Ratepayer Protection Act 417–3, but it only asks states to consider making data centers pay their fair share. This bill makes it a requirement — and adds transparency and clean-energy standards on top.

What the bill does

Three rules for every data center over 50 megawatts.

Fifty megawatts is roughly the power of 40,000 homes. The bill only touches the giants — not small businesses or school server rooms.

1

Show your numbers

Every large data center reports its electricity, peak demand, cooling water, efficiency (PUE), and clean-energy share to the Department of Energy each year — published in a free, searchable public database.

Section 2 →
2

Pay your own way

Data centers cover the full cost of the power plants, substations, and lines built to serve them, sign 12-year contracts with exit fees, and utilities can't raise household rates to cover them.

Section 3 →
3

Clean up over time

New facilities hit a PUE of 1.3; everyone reaches 50% clean energy by 2030 and 100% by 2035, half of it from new sources, and agrees to cut load 10% during grid emergencies.

Who pays? Try it.

The Grid Accountability Fee, in real numbers.

Section 5 funds the whole Act with a fee of $1 per megawatt-hour on large data centers — about one-tenth of one cent per kilowatt-hour. Slide to see what a facility of a given size would pay, and what it would contribute to the public fund.

Based on the 176 million MWh data centers used in 2023, the fee raises roughly $176 million a year: $25 M runs the reporting database, $100 M goes to grid grants in host communities, and the rest rewards companies that beat the standards early.

How it rolls out

A realistic timeline, not an overnight mandate.

Every deadline gives the industry years to adapt, and the Department of Energy can grant a one-time two-year extension to any facility that shows a real plan.

"This bill does not slow down American innovation; it makes sure that innovation does not come at the expense of the people who live next door to it."— Conclusion, WATTS UP Act
  1. Day 180Act takes effect. FERC begins writing the national large-load standard.
  2. Year 1FERC standard finalized; states apply it to their utilities. Data centers pay full interconnection costs.
  3. Year 2First annual energy and water reports are due; public database goes live within 90 days.
  4. 2028New data centers must be designed to a PUE of 1.3 or better.
  5. 203050% clean energy for every large data center.
  6. 2032Existing facilities reach a PUE of 1.5 or better.
  7. 2035100% clean energy, at least half of it from new sources built after the Act.
Yes, the name is on purpose

Congress has passed the USA PATRIOT Act, the CHIPS Act, and the FAIR Act. The WATTS UP Act is just continuing the tradition — and the first word is Water for a reason. Try the button at the top of the page.
